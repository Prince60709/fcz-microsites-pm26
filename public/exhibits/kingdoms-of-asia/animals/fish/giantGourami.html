Giant Gourami

  • Common Names: Common Giant Gourami, Kaloi, Warship Fish.

  • Status: Least Concern / Stable (widely farmed).

  • Range: Native to Southeast Asia (Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, Mekong Basin); introduced globally.

  • Habitat: Warm, slow-moving rivers, swamps, lakes, canals, and flooded marshes with dense vegetation.

  • Size & Weight: 18-28 inches long (up to 30+ inches); up to 22 lbs (10 kg). Lifespan: 10-20+ years in human care.

Physical Description

  • Males: Larger and more streamlined with pointed dorsal and anal fins. They develop a prominent forehead hump with age and turn nearly black during spawning.
  • Females: Smaller with a rounder body and less extended, rounded fins.

Predators & Conservation:

  • Predators: Juveniles are eaten by large birds, crocodilians, otters, and predatory fish. Humans are the primary threat to adults via commercial harvesting.
  • Conservation: While local wild populations face heavy fishing pressure, the species thrives globally through aquaculture. In many Asian cultures, specimens with large head humps are prized as symbols of good luck.

Diet

  • Wild: Mostly herbivorous, eating aquatic plants, fallen fruit, and algae, supplemented by insects, worms, frogs, crustaceans, and small fish.

  • Human Care: Commercial pellets, algae flakes, leafy greens (spinach, romaine), boiled vegetables (carrots, potatoes), legumes, and occasional proteins like bloodworms or earthworms.

Reproduction

  • Nesting: Both sexes weave plant fibers into a floating bubble nest, though the male builds the core structure.

  • Parental Care: After spawning, the female leaves and the male aggressively guards the nest.

  • Development: Eggs hatch in 24-36 hours; fry become free-swimming in 3-5 days.

Behavior & Key Traits

  • Air-Breathing: A specialized labyrinth organ lets them gulp surface air, allowing them to survive in stagnant, low-oxygen water.

  • Temperament: Intelligent and curious, they often recognize their keepers in aquariums. Though generally slow-moving, they become territorial with age and may snap at smaller tankmates.

  • Tactile Feelers: Their pelvic fins are modified into long, thread-like feelers to help navigate murky water.

  • Natural Weed Control: They are frequently introduced to waterways to eat and control invasive aquatic weeds like Salvinia molesta.
